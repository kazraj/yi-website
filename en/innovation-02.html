The Beginning of Your Journey: Discover a Problem Worth Solving

02 / 07

Introduction

Where does innovation begin? Many people would say, "With an idea." But the reality is different.

Every innovation that changed the world started with a real problem that someone noticed and chose not to ignore.

That's why, at YI, we don't begin by designing a solution or building a prototype. We begin by understanding the problem, because a good solution cannot be built on an unclear understanding.

What Will You Learn?

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify the fields that interest you.
  • Observe problems using a structured approach.
  • Distinguish between the real problem and the apparent problem.
  • Identify the people who will benefit from your solution.
  • Evaluate whether a problem is worth working on.

Step 1 | Discover the Field That Interests You

You don't need to know what your project will be from the very beginning.

Start by asking yourself: What subject do I enjoy learning about, even when no one asks me to?

Your interests may include:

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI)
  • Environment and Sustainability
  • Healthcare
  • Education
  • Robotics
  • Engineering
  • Space
  • Cybersecurity
  • Energy
  • Agriculture
  • Sports
  • Arts and Design

Choosing a field doesn't mean you'll stay in it forever. It simply helps you focus and build deeper knowledge over time.

Example

If you're interested in the environment, you might notice problems such as air pollution, water waste, plastic pollution, or high energy consumption.

If you're interested in healthcare, you may notice patients struggling to follow their treatment plans, long waiting times in hospitals, or a lack of supportive solutions for certain patient groups.

Every field contains opportunities that could become meaningful innovations.

Step 2 | Learn to See What Others Overlook

Innovators don't spend all their time searching for ideas. They search for problems.

Start by observing your daily life: at school, at home, on the streets, in hospitals, in the apps you use, or while using transportation.

Then ask yourself:

  • What do people complain about most?
  • What takes longer than it should?
  • What makes a simple task unnecessarily difficult?
  • What could be improved?

Often, the best innovations are hidden in the simplest details.

Real-World Example

Airbnb began when its founders noticed that travelers attending conferences struggled to find places to stay, while many homes and spare rooms remained unused.

They didn't start by building an app. They started by recognizing a real problem.

Step 3 | Don't Fall in Love with Your First Idea

Many new innovators make the same mistake: they think of an idea and immediately start building it.

Instead, pause and ask yourself:

  • Is this the real problem?
  • Why does it happen?
  • Who is affected?
  • Are there existing solutions?
  • If so, why aren't they enough?

Each question helps you understand the problem more deeply and increases your chances of creating a successful innovation.

Step 4 | Identify Your Users

Every successful innovation is designed for a specific group of people.

Before thinking about a solution, ask yourself:

  • Who will use it?
  • How old are they?
  • Where do they spend most of their time?
  • What frustrates them the most?
  • How do they deal with the problem today?

The better you understand your users, the easier it becomes to design an effective solution.

Example

If the problem is poor time management among high school students, then your users are high school students—not everyone. Your solution should be designed around their needs and daily routines.

Step 5 | Define the Scale of the Problem

Not all problems have the same impact. A problem can be:

  • Personal — Affects one person or a small group. Example: difficulty managing daily tasks.
  • Local — Affects a school, neighborhood, or city. Example: parking congestion at a school.
  • National — Affects an entire country. Example: high water or energy consumption.
  • Global — Affects people across many countries. Examples: climate change, air pollution, food security, antibiotic resistance.

Remember, you don't have to start with a global challenge. Many successful innovations began by solving local problems.

Step 6 | Is This Problem Worth Solving?

Before starting your project, ask yourself:

  • Is the problem real, not just an assumption?
  • Does it affect other people?
  • Can I reach the people experiencing it?
  • Can I find reliable information about it?
  • Will I be able to test my solution later?

If most of your answers are yes, you may have found a problem worth turning into an innovation project.

Common Mistakes

  • Designing a solution before understanding the problem.
  • Choosing a problem that affects very few people without a clear reason.
  • Trying to solve multiple unrelated problems in one project.
  • Relying on assumptions instead of observation and research.
  • Copying an existing project without adding new value.

Module Summary

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Understand that every innovation begins with a problem, not an idea.
  • Choose a field that supports your interests and learning.
  • Recognize that observing problems is a skill you can develop.
  • Understand the importance of identifying your users before designing a solution.
  • Evaluate whether a problem is worth developing into an innovation project.

Next Step

You now know which problem you want to solve. But one important question remains: is the problem really what you think it is, or are you only seeing it from your own perspective?

In the next module, you'll learn how to understand your users, build empathy, and gain deeper insights before designing any solution.
